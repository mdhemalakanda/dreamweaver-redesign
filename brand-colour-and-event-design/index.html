Brand Colour and Event Design

Author: Hasan Mahmood Himu

An event is not just a stage, LED screen, banners, lighting, or decoration. A well-planned event creates a complete experience where guests do not simply attend a program—they experience the brand.

This is what we call a Brand Experience.

And when it comes to creating a strong brand experience, colour plays a very important role.

When a guest enters an event venue, they may not read the company name immediately. They may not watch the presentation or listen to the speaker right away. But they will immediately notice the overall colour, lighting, stage design, decoration, and visual environment.

That is why, for an Event Designer, Brand Colour is much more than the colour of a logo. It is an important part of the entire event environment.

What is Brand Colour?

Brand Colour is a specific colour or combination of colours that represents a company or organisation and becomes part of its visual identity.

A company’s primary brand colour could be:

Blue

Red

Green

Black

Orange

Purple

A brand can also have secondary colours and accent colours.

Companies normally use their colour palette across their logo, website, social media, advertising, brochures, business cards, packaging, and other marketing materials.

But event design takes this idea one step further.

The job of an Event Designer is to take the brand colour from a digital or printed format and bring it into a real physical environment.

The brand colour can appear in:

Event entrance

Registration desk

Stage

LED screen

Backdrop

Directional signage

Table setup

Furniture

Lighting

Photo booth

Staff badges or uniforms

Exhibition areas

Welcome areas

Networking spaces

When these elements follow the same colour language and design direction, the entire event feels connected and professionally planned.

Why is Brand Colour Important in Event Design?

Colour is one of the first visual elements people notice. It can also influence how people feel about a space and a brand.

For example:

Blue

Blue is often associated with trust, stability, technology, security, and professionalism.

This is why blue is commonly used in corporate conferences, technology events, business meetings, and professional gatherings.

Red

Red can communicate energy, excitement, passion, and attention.

It can be very effective when an event needs a strong and energetic visual presence.

Green

Green can be associated with growth, sustainability, nature, freshness, and new opportunities.

It can work particularly well for environmental, agriculture, sustainability, health, and development-related events.

Black

Black can create a premium, powerful, elegant, and sophisticated feeling.

It can work very well for luxury events, award ceremonies, fashion events, and premium corporate gatherings.

White

White can create a clean, modern, simple, and spacious appearance.

It can also help other brand colours stand out more clearly.

However, there is one important thing to remember:

Choosing the right colour is only the beginning of good event design.

The real question is:

How should that colour be used?

Using Colours Similar to the Brand Colour

Suppose a company’s primary brand colour is Blue.

Instead of using only one shade of blue, an Event Designer can create a colour palette using different shades.

For example:

Navy Blue + Royal Blue + Light Blue + White + Grey

This type of colour combination can create a clean, professional, and harmonious environment.

It can work particularly well for:

Corporate conferences

Seminars

AGM events

Product launches

Award ceremonies

Business meetings

Corporate celebrations

Imagine entering a corporate event venue.

You see a dark blue stage background, different shades of blue on the LED screen, soft blue lighting on the walls, blue and white branding at the registration desk, and neutral-coloured furniture.

Everything feels connected.

Nothing looks random or out of place.

The venue starts to feel like one complete visual identity.

However, using too much of the same colour can create another problem.

If the entire venue is covered in the same shade of blue, the design may look flat, repetitive, or boring.

This is where an experienced Event Designer makes a difference.

By combining different shades, brightness levels, textures, neutral colours, and lighting, we can create visual depth without losing the brand identity.

How Can Contrast Colours Make an Event More Attractive?

There is no rule that says an event must use only colours similar to the brand colour.

Sometimes, using a carefully selected contrast colour can make an event much more attractive.

For a blue brand, for example, we could use:

Blue + Orange

or

Blue + Gold

or

Blue + White + Warm Amber Lighting

In these combinations, Blue maintains the brand identity while the contrasting colour creates visual excitement and attention.

Contrast can be particularly effective in stage design.

Imagine a corporate event where the LED screen has a dark blue background. Warm amber lighting is then used around the speaker area.

The blue maintains the corporate identity, while the warm lighting creates depth and a premium atmosphere.

The result is a stage that is not only branded but also visually sophisticated.

However, there is an important difference between contrast and using too many colours.

Contrast does not mean using many colours together.

A good contrast should be planned, controlled, and suitable for the brand.

Using the 60-30-10 Rule in Event Design

A popular concept used in interior and event design is the 60-30-10 Rule.

It is a simple way to think about colour balance.

It is not a strict formula that must be followed in every event. Instead, it provides a useful guideline for creating a balanced visual environment.

60% — Dominant Colour

The dominant colour creates the visual foundation of the event.

It can appear in:

Venue walls

Flooring

Large backdrops

Stage structures

Ceiling treatment

Neutral décor

Large environmental elements

For a corporate event, colours such as white, black, grey, beige, or other neutral tones can be used as the foundation.

30% — Secondary Colour

The secondary colour supports the primary brand colour.

It can be used in:

Stage elements

Furniture

Registration counters

Table linen

Signage

Decorative structures

Branding elements

10% — Accent Colour

This is where visual attention can be created.

Accent colours can be used in:

Stage lighting

LED highlights

Floral décor

Neon elements

Photo booths

Decorative elements

Small branding details

Again, the 60-30-10 ratio does not need to be followed exactly.

The main goal is to create visual balance.

Brand Colour Does Not Mean Everything Has to Be That Colour

One of the most common mistakes in corporate event design is thinking:

“Our brand colour is red, so everything has to be red.”

Professional event design does not work that way.

An experienced Event Designer does not simply cover the entire venue with the brand colour. Instead, the colour is used strategically.

Suppose the brand colour is Red.

Instead of making the entire venue red, we can use red in:

Selected stage elements

LED graphics

Architectural lighting

Registration branding

Staff badges

Directional signage

Photo zones

Small branding details

The rest of the environment can use black, white, grey, or other neutral colours.

This can actually make the red look more powerful.

Sometimes, the less an accent colour is used, the more attention it receives.

Good event design is not about using more colour.

It is about using the right colour in the right place.

Brand Colour in Stage Design

The stage is often the most important visual area of a corporate event.

But when designing a stage, we cannot think about brand colour alone.

We need to consider:

Stage + LED + Lighting + Branding + Speaker + Camera

A colour that looks one way on a printed banner may look completely different on an LED screen or under stage lighting.

For example, if a very bright red LED background is combined with strong red stage lighting, the entire stage may become too intense.

Instead, we could use:

Darker red LED graphics

Black or neutral stage structures

Controlled red lighting

White typography

Selected red accent elements

This keeps the brand identity while making the stage look more balanced and premium.

Stage design should also consider how the event will appear in photographs and videos.

A stage may look good in person but not look good on camera if the lighting and colours are not properly balanced.

That is why modern event design must consider both the live experience and the camera experience.

Event Branding Is Not Limited to the Stage

A common mistake is to focus all branding efforts on the main stage.

A strong event design considers the entire guest journey.

For example:

Entrance → Registration → Welcome Area → Main Hall → Stage → Networking Area → Photo Zone → Exit

Each part of this journey can use the same colour language and design elements.

For example, the entrance may introduce the brand colour.

The registration desk can continue the same visual style.

Directional signs can use the same colour palette.

The main stage can take the colour concept to a larger scale.

The photo zone can continue the same design language.

This creates a consistent experience from the moment guests arrive until they leave.

Guests will not feel that different branding materials have simply been placed around the venue.

Instead, they will feel:

The entire venue is part of the brand experience.

The Relationship Between Colour, Lighting and LED

Colour and lighting are closely connected in event design.

A colour shown in a brand guideline will not always look exactly the same on an LED screen, printed banner, RGB light, or architectural lighting.

This is why an Event Designer needs to consider several factors before finalising a colour concept.

These include:

Venue lighting

LED screen brightness

Stage lighting

Camera colour capture

Photography

Existing venue wall colours

Furniture colours

Daytime or nighttime event

Indoor or outdoor venue

Guest movement areas

Camera position and distance from the stage

For corporate events, award ceremonies, product launches, and large conferences, photography and video are especially important.

Event photographs and videos may later be used on:

Social media

Websites

Press releases

Company reports

Marketing campaigns

Internal communications

So the colour concept should look good not only to guests in the venue but also through a camera.

Premium Event Design Does Not Mean More Colours

Many people think that an event becomes more attractive when more colours are used.

In reality, premium event design often works in the opposite way.

The strength of premium design is control.

Knowing where to use colour—and where not to use it—is what creates a sophisticated environment.

For example, a Black & White venue with carefully selected Red accents can create a powerful corporate look.

For a youth event, however, vibrant and contrasting colours may be more appropriate.

For a luxury event:

Black + Gold

can create an elegant atmosphere.

For a technology event:

Blue + White + Cool Lighting

can create a modern and professional environment.

For an energetic youth event, bright and vibrant colours may be the better choice.

This means:

One colour combination does not work for every event.

The colour strategy should depend on:

Event purpose

Target audience

Venue

Time of day

Brand personality

Event format

Desired guest experience

Lighting requirements

Photography and video requirements

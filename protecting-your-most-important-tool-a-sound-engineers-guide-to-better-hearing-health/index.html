Protecting Your Most Important Tool: A Sound Engineer’s Guide to Better Hearing Health

For a sound engineer, hearing is more than a sense—it is the foundation of the profession.

Every day, sound engineers make critical decisions based on what they hear: adjusting EQ, controlling feedback, balancing vocals and instruments, setting monitor levels, tuning PA systems, checking microphones, mixing broadcasts, and judging the overall character of a performance. But the same working environment that requires exceptional hearing can also expose engineers to high sound levels for long periods.

Concerts, festivals, rehearsals, sound checks, stage monitors, subwoofers, headphones, in-ear monitors, and loud production environments can all contribute to cumulative noise exposure. Repeated exposure to loud sound can cause noise-induced hearing loss and tinnitus, and the damage can be permanent. The encouraging part is that noise-induced hearing loss is largely preventable when exposure is managed properly.

For sound engineers, protecting hearing should therefore be considered part of professional practice—not an optional safety measure.

Why Hearing Health Matters So Much for Sound Engineers

A sound engineer depends on the ability to recognize small differences in frequency, dynamics, distortion, stereo image, clarity, transients, and tonal balance. Hearing fatigue can affect those judgments even before permanent hearing damage becomes obvious.

Loud sound exposure can temporarily cause muffled hearing or ringing in the ears. These symptoms may improve after the ears recover, but repeated or prolonged exposure can eventually cause irreversible damage to the sensory cells and structures involved in hearing.

This creates a professional risk. A hearing system that is repeatedly overexposed may gradually become less sensitive, particularly at higher frequencies. NIOSH notes that noise-induced hearing loss initially tends to affect hearing in the 3,000–6,000 Hz region, which is especially important for professionals whose work depends on detailed high-frequency perception.

The goal is not to avoid loud sound completely. The goal is to control the combination of level, duration, distance, and frequency of exposure.

Understand the Relationship Between Sound Level and Exposure Time

One of the most important principles for an engineer is simple: louder sound requires less exposure time.

WHO states that, for leisure listening, 80 dB can be listened to for up to 40 hours per week under its safe-listening reference, while increasing the level significantly reduces the recommended listening time. WHO gives 90 dB as approximately four hours per week.

For occupational exposure, NIOSH uses a more conservative reference of 85 dBA over an eight-hour workday, using a 3 dB exchange rate. Under that approach, every 3 dB increase cuts the allowable exposure time in half: 85 dBA for 8 hours, 88 dBA for 4 hours, 91 dBA for 2 hours, 94 dBA for 1 hour, and 100 dBA for 15 minutes.

These figures should not be treated as a target for mixing or as permission to work continuously at those levels. They demonstrate how quickly noise exposure accumulates.

For sound engineers, the practical lesson is clear: do not judge hearing safety only by how loud the system sounds at one moment. Think about your total exposure over the entire shift.

Measure Sound Instead of Guessing

Professional sound engineers are already comfortable measuring levels for technical reasons. The same discipline should be applied to hearing protection.

A sound level meter can tell you how loud the environment is, but a personal noise dosimeter can be more useful for an engineer because exposure can vary dramatically throughout the day. An engineer may spend time at front of house, backstage, near side fills, at monitors, during sound check, and inside production areas—all with different sound exposure levels.

NIOSH recommends understanding both how loud an area is and how much noise actually reaches the worker.

A sensible approach is to monitor:

FOH position: Check the average and peak levels at the mixing position.

Monitor world: Pay particular attention to engineers working close to wedges, side fills, or drum and guitar monitoring systems.

Stage exposure: Measure the areas where you regularly stand during sound checks and performances.

Load-in and load-out: Remember that amplifying equipment is not the only source of noise. Generators, machinery, road cases, rigging activity, and production equipment can also contribute to exposure.

Personal exposure: Consider using an occupational noise dosimeter during particularly demanding production days.

Modern smartphones can also provide useful screening measurements. WHO specifically notes that sound-level apps, including the NIOSH Sound Level Meter app, can help people monitor sound levels. However, smartphone applications should be viewed as practical screening tools rather than substitutes for professional measurement equipment when accurate occupational assessment is required.

Use the “Lowest Effective Level” Principle

A common mistake in live sound is assuming that louder automatically means better.

In reality, better system tuning, improved coverage, cleaner gain structure, controlled dynamics, appropriate microphone selection, good system alignment, and effective stage monitoring can often improve clarity without simply increasing volume.

A skilled engineer should aim for the lowest effective sound level that delivers the required artistic and technical result.

This approach benefits everyone: the engineer, performers, crew, and audience.

Instead of immediately increasing the master output, examine the cause of the problem.

Is the vocal unclear because of excessive low-mid buildup?

Is the monitor too quiet because of poor microphone placement?

Is intelligibility being lost because of excessive stage volume?

Is feedback limiting the system because the microphone and speaker positions are poorly arranged?

Is the PA coverage uneven, forcing some areas to be louder than necessary?

Solving these problems technically is often more effective than simply turning up the system.

Increase Distance From Loudspeakers Whenever Possible

Distance is one of the simplest ways to reduce exposure.

OSHA notes that, in open space, doubling the distance from a sound source can reduce sound exposure by approximately 6 dBA.

This principle is highly relevant to sound engineers.

You may need to work with loudspeakers, subwoofers, stage monitors, and amplifier systems, but you do not necessarily need to stand directly beside them for extended periods.

Avoid unnecessary time directly in front of loudspeaker arrays or close to high-output stage sources. During pauses in production, move to a quieter area rather than remaining beside the loudest equipment simply because it is part of your workstation.

Even small increases in distance can make a meaningful difference.

Treat Sound Checks as Part of Your Daily Noise Dose

Sound checks are sometimes overlooked because there is no audience and the performance has not started yet.

For hearing health, however, sound check is still exposure.

A typical event day may involve several stages of high-volume audio:

Rig testing → line check → monitor check → full sound check → rehearsal → doors open → performance → encore → breakdown.

The total exposure from all these periods matters.

A sound engineer who works through a full sound check at high levels and then spends several hours mixing a loud performance may accumulate substantial exposure before the day is finished.

Consider reducing unnecessary playback time during testing. Once a technical issue has been identified, there is little benefit in leaving the system running loudly while discussing how to fix it.

Give Your Ears Quiet Recovery Periods

Hearing protection is not only about what happens during loud periods. It is also about what happens between them.

WHO recommends taking regular breaks from loud sound and specifically advises people attending loud venues to spend time in quieter environments.

For sound engineers, this means using natural gaps in the production schedule intelligently.

After a loud sound check, spend the break away from the PA.

During meal breaks, choose the quietest practical location.

When a production issue is being discussed away from the active system, reduce or mute unnecessary playback.

Avoid spending your off-duty hours listening to additional loud music through headphones or attending another loud venue immediately after a long production shift.

Your ears need recovery just as other parts of your body do.

Choose Hearing Protection Designed for Music Professionals

Traditional hearing protection can reduce sound, but poorly selected protection may make music sound unbalanced and can make communication difficult.

For sound engineers, musician’s earplugs or professionally fitted hearing protection may be preferable because they can provide more consistent attenuation across frequencies while maintaining a more natural sound balance.

The exact protector should depend on the work environment and required communication.

For extremely high-noise environments, higher-attenuation protection may be necessary. In other situations, lower-attenuation filtered earplugs may offer a better balance between protection and communication.

The important point is that hearing protection must be:

Appropriate for the exposure.

Properly fitted.

Worn consistently.

Used correctly.

NIOSH emphasizes that hearing protection is most effective when it is selected appropriately, fits correctly, and is used consistently. It also recommends reducing noise through engineering and administrative controls rather than relying on personal protective equipment alone.

Do Not Forget In-Ear Monitors

In-ear monitors can be an extremely useful professional tool, but they introduce a different type of hearing-risk challenge.

Because the sound source is close to the ear, excessively high IEM levels can create substantial exposure without the engineer or performer necessarily realizing how loud the signal is.

Engineers should therefore:

Keep IEM levels as low as practical.

Avoid using excessive volume to compensate for poor isolation.

Use properly fitted IEMs.

Check mixes at moderate levels whenever possible.

Take regular listening breaks.

Pay attention to complaints of ear fatigue or ringing.

For IEM monitoring, good isolation is particularly important. When external noise is reduced, the listener does not need to raise the monitoring level simply to hear the desired signal.

Be Careful With Headphones in the Studio

Sound engineers often spend hours editing, mixing, checking cues, preparing show files, or monitoring broadcasts through headphones.

Headphones can create a false sense of safety because the environment may feel quiet even while the ears are receiving high sound levels.

WHO recommends keeping personal-device volume below 60% of maximum as a practical precaution and using well-fitted or noise-cancelling headphones to reduce the temptation to increase volume in noisy environments.

For professional work, do not rely exclusively on a fixed percentage such as “50%” or “60%,” because device output and headphone sensitivity vary. Instead, use an appropriate monitoring level and take frequent breaks.

A useful professional habit is to perform detailed editing or mixing at a moderate level and periodically check the material at lower levels. This can also improve mixing decisions by preventing the ear from adapting to an unnecessarily loud monitoring level.
